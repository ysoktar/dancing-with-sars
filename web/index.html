      Teaching a computer to read radar

      Three complementary approaches, all trained on the fly on the site’s real NISAR stack. No server, no pre-trained black box: the code runs on your GPU through TensorFlow.js and WebGL.

      01

      Unsupervised scattering classes

      k-means++ clustering on six per-pixel features: HH, HV, HV−HH contrast, temporal mean and temporal variability. Each class is then read physically: surface, volume or double-bounce scattering, or dynamic.

      Press Run k-means.

      02

      Change detection: log-ratio + Otsu

      The standard SAR change detector. The log of the intensity ratio between two dates makes speckle noise roughly additive. Otsu’s method then picks the threshold automatically, by maximizing the variance between the “changed” and “unchanged” classes.

      Pick two dates.

      03

      Deep autoencoder: learning what “normal” looks like

      A neural network (D → 48 → 16 → 2 → 16 → 48 → D) learns to compress each pixel’s full temporal signature (HH and HV on every date) into just two numbers, then rebuild it. It learns the common rhythms of the scene. Pixels it cannot rebuild behave unusually: clearings, new water, fresh lava, fire scars. The 2-D bottleneck also gives a learned “map” of temporal behaviours.

      Radar 101

      How does a radar “see” the surface?

      NISAR sends its own microwave pulses and listens for the echo. That is active remote sensing, so it works at night and through clouds. The echo strength depends mainly on two things: roughness and the dielectric constant (essentially, wetness).

      Wavelength decides what you see

      Three ways to scatter

      SurfaceSmooth water reflects the pulse away like a mirror, so it looks dark. Rough soil sends more energy back. Shows mainly in HH.
      Double-bounceGround plus a wall, or water plus a tree trunk, makes a corner reflector. Very bright HH. Signature of cities and flooded forest.
      VolumeInside a canopy or dry snow the wave scatters many times and its polarization gets scrambled. Strong HV (cross-pol).

      Polarization: H and V

      NISAR transmits horizontally (H) polarized waves and receives both H and V. HH (sent H, received H) responds to surfaces and corners. HV responds only when something randomizes the wave, which makes it a direct proxy for vegetation volume and biomass. In this site’s colours, magenta = HH-dominant, green = HV-dominant, and white means both channels are strong.

      NISAR at a glance

      • Launch 30 July 2025, GSLV-F16 from Sriharikota, India
      • Orbit 747 km, sun-synchronous, dawn–dusk, 98.4° inclination
      • Repeat every 12 days, ascending and descending
      • Radars L-band (24 cm, NASA JPL) and S-band (10 cm, ISRO)
      • Antenna 12 m deployable mesh reflector with SweepSAR
      • Swath about 242 km; resolution 3–10 m; global land and ice every 12 days
      • Products RSLC → GSLC, GCOV (backscatter, used here), GUNW (interferograms), GOFF (pixel offsets)

      Data & methods

      Open data, open code, honest limits

      Where the data comes from

      1. Search: the ASF DAAC search API returns every NISAR L2 GCOV granule that intersects each site.
      2. Track lock: we keep the single path/frame/direction that covers the site most often, so every date has the same viewing geometry.
      3. Imagery: the geocoded browse image published with each granule (*_LATLON.png, R = HH, G = HV) is cropped to the site window.
      4. Co-registration: provisional products ship without overlay bounds, so each date is aligned to a reference by FFT phase correlation. Shifts are shown per acquisition.
      5. Global density: NASA CMR granule footprints, sampled four days per month, binned on a 3° grid.
      6. Refresh: a GitHub Actions workflow re-runs the pipeline weekly and redeploys this site.

      Limitations

      • Browse images are 8-bit display products, not calibrated γ⁰ backscatter. Every number here is relative brightness. For calibrated values, run pipeline/fetch_hdf5.py with a free NASA Earthdata login.
      • Browse resolution (~150 m) is much coarser than the full 10–20 m GCOV product.
      • Many products are labelled provisional or beta by the NISAR project and may be reprocessed later.
      • Ground deformation (subsidence, volcanic inflation) needs interferometric GUNW products and is described here as a hypothesis, not measured.
      • The ML models are unsupervised and exploratory. They point to places worth a closer look; they are not validated land-cover maps.
